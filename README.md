# econ3916-lab03-visualization
# Honest vs. Misleading Visualizations

## Objective

An examination of how identical data supports contradictory conclusions depending on presentation choices, using Tufte's Lie Factor to quantify distortion and a systematic exploratory checklist to establish what a dataset will and will not support.

## Data

Anscombe's Quartet (four constructed datasets, eleven observations each); a synthetic quarterly revenue series used to demonstrate axis truncation; Average Hourly Earnings of Production and Nonsupervisory Employees (FRED `AHETPI`, monthly from 1964) deflated by the Consumer Price Index (`CPIAUCSL`) to constant 2020 dollars; and the World Bank GDP panel — 13,979 rows across 4 columns, covering 262 country and aggregate codes from 1960 to 2023.

## Methodology

- Reproduced Anscombe's Quartet and confirmed that four datasets agreeing to two decimal places on mean, variance, and correlation diverge completely under visual inspection, establishing that summary statistics do not identify a distribution.
- Quantified axis distortion using Tufte's Lie Factor, defined as the proportional effect displayed in the graphic divided by the proportional effect present in the data. A four-percent revenue increase plotted against an axis truncated at 96 units yields on-screen bar heights of two and six, a displayed effect of 200 percent, and a Lie Factor of 49.
- Redesigned the same series as an annotated dot plot drawn from zero, demonstrating that a modest change can be made legible through labelling rather than through axis manipulation.
- Constructed four presentations of one deflated wage series — full range from zero, truncated axis, restricted time window, and logarithmic scale — to isolate which distortions arise from the axis and which from sample selection.
- Executed a four-stage exploratory protocol on the World Bank panel: structure (shape, dtypes, cardinality), distribution (raw against log-transformed histograms, skewness), relationship (trajectories for five major economies), and anomaly (distribution by decade, missingness heatmap).
- Built an interactive Lie Factor explorer with controls for series, axis floor, date window, and scale. Its calculation was validated by evaluating its function against the revenue figures computed by hand earlier in the lab, which returned a Lie Factor of 49 as expected, and by confirming that a zero floor over the full range returns a Lie Factor of 1.00.

## Key Findings

**Summary statistics are insufficient to characterise a dataset.** Anscombe's four datasets share their first two moments and their correlation coefficient while exhibiting a linear relationship, a curve, a linear relationship distorted by a single outlier, and a vertical cluster with one leverage point. A regression fitted to any of them returns effectively the same line. Visual inspection is not a presentational courtesy but a diagnostic requirement.

**Axis truncation distorts by a large and calculable factor.** The revenue example produces a Lie Factor of 49 — the graphic presents a four-percent change as a two-hundred-percent change. The distortion arises because truncation subtracts a constant from every plotted height, leaving the absolute difference unchanged while collapsing the base against which it is read.

**Real earnings show a pattern that the nominal series conceals entirely.** Deflated to 2020 dollars, average hourly earnings move from $20.92 in 1964 to $25.20 in 2026 — roughly twenty percent across six decades — peaking near $24.12 in 1973, falling to a trough near $19.71 in 1995, and standing at only $22.96 by 2015. The nominal series over the same period rises from $2.50 to $32.53, a thirteen-fold increase that reflects the declining value of the dollar rather than any improvement in purchasing power.

**Sample selection distorts without touching the axis.** The panel restricted to 2015 onward is drawn from zero on an untruncated axis and is, by Tufte's measure, entirely honest. It nonetheless conceals four decades of declining real wages by excluding them. Axis-based measures of distortion do not detect this class of error, nor do they detect the unit error embedded in plotting a nominal series across decades — a nominal chart drawn from zero returns a Lie Factor near 1.00 while implying a thirteen-fold gain in worker welfare.

**Raw GDP is unreadable without transformation.** The untransformed distribution has a skewness of 8.4, concentrating nearly all observations in the leftmost bin while a handful of large economies occupy the remaining range. Under a base-ten logarithm the skewness falls to 0.2 and the distribution becomes legible. The transformation works because GDP is generated by approximately proportional growth, so its logarithm is closer to symmetric — the same reason a log axis is the honest default for compounding quantities.

**Missingness in the GDP panel is structural rather than random.** Coverage is sparse at both ends of the period and complete in between: approximately 85 percent of the sampled countries are absent in 1960 and approximately 25 percent in 2023. The early gaps reflect the progressive expansion of national statistical capacity and World Bank reporting coverage; the recent gaps reflect publication lag. Neither is independent of the values that would have been recorded, since the countries missing in 1960 are disproportionately those with the least developed statistical systems and the lowest incomes.

## Limitations

The Lie Factor measures distortion introduced by the mapping from data to graphical height. It is silent on distortion introduced by sample selection, by choice of series, or by unit inconsistency, all of which are demonstrated in this lab to be equally capable of reversing a conclusion. It is also not well defined on a logarithmic axis, where height encodes proportional rather than absolute change; a height-based ratio computed there is not interpretable on the same scale and should not be reported as though it were. `AHETPI` covers production and nonsupervisory employees and reports a mean rather than a median, and is therefore sensitive to compositional change in the employed population.
